PARTI.

What makes learning unsupervised

In Unit 1 every row came with an answer — the loan defaulted or it didn't. Here the answer column is gone. The algorithm must find structure with nothing to check itself against.

THE SHIFT Supervised learning maps inputs to a known output. Unsupervised learning has inputs only — it asks "what is the shape of this data?"

No labels, no answer key

A label is the thing you're trying to predict — the default flag, the price, the fraud tag. Supervised models learn by comparing their guess to that label. Unsupervised models never see one. So they can't be "right" or "wrong" in the same way; instead they surface structure — natural groupings, hidden dimensions, co-occurring items — that a human then interprets and names.

This changes three things at once. There is no train/test split in the usual sense, because there's no answer to hold out. There is no accuracy or AUC, because those need a truth column. And the human is back in the loop at the end: the algorithm says "here are three groups," and the decision-maker decides what those groups mean.

Three jobs unsupervised learning does

Clustering puts similar rows together — customers who behave alike, merchants with the same risk profile, transactions that look normal (and the few that don't). Dimensionality reduction squeezes many correlated columns into a few informative ones — turning eight correlated return series into two market factors. Association-rule mining finds items that travel together — the products a customer tends to hold at the same time.

Why finance leans on it

Most financial data arrives unlabelled. Nobody has hand-tagged every customer's "true segment" or every transaction's "true anomaly." Unsupervised methods work with what you have — the raw behaviour — which is exactly why they power segmentation, risk factor models, anomaly screens and cross-sell engines.

How you grade a model with no answer key

Every metric here is internal — computed from the geometry of the result, not from a truth column:

Silhouette (−1 to 1) asks, for each point, "am I closer to my own group than to the nearest other group?" Near 1 = tight, well-separated clusters. Inertia is the total squared distance of points to their cluster centre — lower is tighter, and its "elbow" as k grows hints at the right number of clusters. Davies–Bouldin compares within-cluster spread to between-cluster distance — lower is better. For dimensionality reduction, explained variance says how much of the data's spread your few components kept. For association rules, support, confidence and lift measure how common, how reliable, and how surprising a pattern is.

The mindset to carry into the lab: the algorithm proposes structure; you dispose of it. A cluster is only useful once a human looks at it and says "ah — these are our dormant high-net-worth customers."
PARTII.

Clustering — grouping by similarity three ways

Clustering answers one question — "which rows belong together?" — but the three algorithms disagree on what "together" means, and the disagreement is the lesson.

A NOTE All three need features on a comparable scale. Standardize first, or the largest-magnitude column silently decides every grouping.

1 · K-Means — k centres, nearest wins

You tell K-Means how many groups you want (k). It drops k centre-points at random, assigns every row to its nearest centre, moves each centre to the average of its members, and repeats until nothing moves. Fast, simple, and the default first reach — but you must choose k, and it assumes clusters are roughly round and similar-sized.

minimise   Σ ‖ xᵢ − c(xᵢ) ‖² Add up how far every point sits from its own cluster centre, squared, and make that total (the "inertia") as small as possible. Each round: assign points to the nearest centre, then slide each centre to the middle of its points.

Finance use: customer and portfolio segmentation, where you want a fixed number of clean buckets to act on. Explained like you're in 6th class: drop k flags on a playground, send every kid to the nearest flag, move each flag to the middle of its group, repeat until the flags stop moving.

2 · Hierarchical — build a family tree

Start with every row as its own tiny cluster. Repeatedly glue the two closest clusters together, and keep going until everything is one big cluster. The record of merges is a dendrogram — a tree you can "cut" at any height to get however many clusters you want. You don't pre-commit to k, and you get the nested structure for free; the cost is speed on large data.

d(A, B) = average distance between points of A and points of B "Linkage" is just how you measure the gap between two groups: the average pair distance (average linkage), the farthest pair (complete), or the closest pair (single). Merge the two groups with the smallest gap, then recompute.

Finance use: merchant/branch tiering and asset grouping, where the nesting matters — sub-segments inside segments. 6th-class version: keep holding hands with your nearest neighbour until the whole class is one chain; the order you joined up tells you who's most alike.

3 · DBSCAN — follow the density, flag the loners

DBSCAN doesn't need k. It grows a cluster wherever points are packed densely (at least minPts neighbours within radius eps), walks outward through the dense region, and stops at the edges. Anything not reachable from a dense core is labelled noise. That noise label is the feature: it finds the outliers you didn't know to look for.

point is core if   |{ p : ‖p − point‖ ≤ eps }| ≥ minPts A point is a "core" if it has at least minPts neighbours inside radius eps. Clusters are chains of overlapping cores; points reachable from a core join it; everything else is noise.

Finance use: anomaly and novel-fraud detection, where the interesting rows are precisely the ones that fit no group. 6th-class version: a group is any crowd standing close together; anyone standing alone in a field is not in a group — they're the odd one out worth a second look.

Which clustering, when

K-Means when you want a fixed number of tidy buckets and clusters are blob-like. Hierarchical when you want to see the nesting and choose k after looking. DBSCAN when clusters are odd-shaped or, more often, when the point is to catch the outliers.

PARTIII.

Dimensionality reduction — fewer columns, same story

When columns are highly correlated, most of them are saying the same thing twice. Dimensionality reduction rewrites the data in a smaller set of columns that keep the meaning.

A NOTE PCA ignores labels; LDA uses them. That single difference is why PCA is unsupervised and LDA sits on the border.

4 · PCA — the directions of greatest variance

Imagine eight correlated return series. They mostly move together — when the market rises, most rise. PCA finds the single direction along which the data varies the most (the first principal component), then the next-most-varying direction at right angles to it, and so on. Keep the first two or three and you've compressed eight columns into a handful that still capture most of the movement.

C = covariance(X)  →  eigenvectors of C = the new axes Build the covariance matrix (how every pair of features moves together). Its eigenvectors point along the directions of greatest spread; each eigenvalue says how much variance lies along its direction. Keep the biggest few.

Finance use: risk factor models, yield-curve compression (level/slope/curvature fall straight out of PCA), and de-noising before another model. 6th-class version: if everyone's height and arm-span rise together, you barely need both — one "size" number carries almost the whole story. PCA finds that "size" number for you.

5 · LDA — the projection that separates known groups

Linear Discriminant Analysis has the same output as PCA — a few new axes — but a different goal. Where PCA maximises overall spread ignoring any labels, LDA uses group labels and finds the projection that pushes the group means as far apart as possible while keeping each group tight. It's the one technique in this unit that needs a label, which is why it's the bridge from unsupervised to supervised.

maximise   between-group spread ÷ within-group spread Find the direction w that makes the group averages far apart (big numerator) while each group stays compact along it (small denominator). Solve it as the top eigenvectors of Sᵥᵥ⁻¹Sᵦ — within-group scatter inverted, times between-group scatter.

Finance use: visualising and sharpening pre-defined segments — risk bands, rating classes — and as a feature-compression step ahead of a classifier. 6th-class version: PCA finds the angle that best shows how spread out the whole class is; LDA finds the angle that best shows the boys standing apart from the girls.

PCA vs LDA in one line

PCA asks "which directions vary most?" (no labels). LDA asks "which directions best separate these known groups?" (labels required). Same shape of answer, opposite question.

PARTIV.

Association rules — what goes with what

The last family isn't about geometry at all. It's about co-occurrence: which things show up together often enough, and reliably enough, to act on.

A NOTE Confidence can lie. A rule can be 90% confident and still worthless if the outcome is 90% common anyway. Lift is the honest metric.

6 · Apriori — frequent itemsets, then rules

Each customer holds a basket of products. Apriori first finds the itemsets that appear in at least a minimum fraction of baskets (their support), using a clever shortcut: if an item is rare, any pair containing it is at least as rare, so you never bother counting it. From the frequent sets it forms rules like "has a credit card → also has insurance," and scores each one.

support = P(A and B)  ·  confidence = P(B | A)  ·  lift = confidence ÷ P(B) Support = how often the combo shows up. Confidence = of the people with A, how many also have B. Lift = how much more likely B is when A is present, versus B's baseline — lift > 1 means a real pull, lift = 1 means coincidence.

Finance use: next-best-offer and cross-sell engines, product-bundling, and spotting which services anchor a relationship. 6th-class version: if almost everyone who buys bread also buys butter — and butter isn't something everyone buys anyway — then bread genuinely "pulls" butter. Put them near each other.

Reading a rule

High support = common enough to matter. High confidence = reliable when the trigger is present. High lift = genuinely surprising, not just two popular things co-occurring. A great cross-sell rule scores well on all three.

LAB01.

Build a clustering pipeline

Drag nodes from the palette into the pipeline (or click a node to drop it in the next free slot). Choose a dataset, prepare it for distance, pick K-Means, Hierarchical or DBSCAN, and evaluate — with no answer key. Run the same data through two algorithms; the disagreement is the lesson.

📖 Lab guide — preparing for distance, the three algorithms, and what to combine read first ▾

Every clustering algorithm here runs on distance. Whatever makes two rows look "far apart" decides the groups — so cleaning is not housekeeping, it is the model. Cleaning runs in order; stack as many steps as you need.

Standardize (z-score) Recentres each feature at mean 0, std 1. Non-negotiable for clustering — without it, income in thousands out-shouts a 1–10 digital score and becomes the only thing that matters. Use on every numeric clustering pipeline. Leave it out once, on purpose, to see a single column hijack the segments.
Normalize (min-max) Squeezes each feature into [0, 1]. Same goal as z-score, but one extreme value compresses everybody else into a sliver of the range. Use when features are bounded and outlier-free (shares, ratios, scores).
Log-transform skew Applies log(x+1) to right-skewed, non-negative features — ticket size, volumes, deposits. Stops a few giants from stretching the distance scale for everyone else. Use before scaling on money-sized columns. Log after z-scoring does nothing (values are already negative).
Drop duplicates Duplicated CRM or fund records double-count a point and pull a centroid towards it. Use when the data came from joins or extracts — which is almost always.
Drop missing rows / Mean impute Distance can't be computed with a hole in the row. Drop the row, or fill with the column mean (which parks the customer in the middle of that feature). Drop when nulls are few; impute when every customer must receive a segment.
Remove outliers (IQR) Drops rows more than 1.5×IQR beyond the quartiles. In segmentation a few extremes can hijack a whole cluster — trimming helps. In anomaly detection the extremes are the product. Never before DBSCAN on the transaction or journal data — you would delete exactly what you were looking for.
PCA → 2 components / 90% variance Optional Features step: compress correlated columns into a few principal components, then cluster there. Strips noise dimensions and often raises the silhouette. Use when several columns say the same thing twice. Profile the clusters in original units afterwards (the results do this for you).
The canonical order
dedup→ drop / impute nulls→ outliers (segmentation only)→ log-transform→ scale→ PCA (optional)

There is no train/test split in this lab: with no answer key there is nothing to hold out. The model is judged on the geometry of its own result.

Three algorithms, three definitions of "belongs together". Each node carries its knobs — edit them in the pipeline before you run.

K-Means · k Places k centres, assigns every row to the nearest, moves each centre to the mean of its rows, repeats. Runs 10 random starts and keeps the tightest — like scikit-learn. Assumes round, similar-sized groups. You must choose k. Use the k-scan evaluation to see the elbow and the silhouette for k = 2…8.
Hierarchical · k · linkage Every row starts alone; the two closest groups merge, again and again, until one remains. The merge record is a tree you cut at k. Linkage defines "closest": average (mean pair distance), complete (farthest pair), single (nearest pair — prone to chaining), ward (smallest rise in within-group variance). Use when nesting matters — tiers inside tiers. The merge-distance chart shows where a natural cut sits.
DBSCAN · eps · minPts A point with at least minPts neighbours inside radius eps is a core; clusters grow through chains of cores; anything unreachable is noise. No k needed. Use when the goal is the loners. Lower eps → more noise flagged. The eps-scan shows the k-distance curve — its knee is the natural eps.
Reading the evaluation — no answer key

Silhouette (−1 to 1): for each row, "am I closer to my own group than to the next one?" Above 0.5 = clear structure; 0.25–0.5 = real but overlapping; below 0.25 = weak. Davies–Bouldin: average ratio of within-group spread to between-group distance — lower is better, under 0.8 is good. Inertia: total squared distance to group centres — always falls as k rises, so look for the elbow, not the minimum.

Cluster quality shows those numbers plus cluster sizes (or, for hierarchical, the merge distances). k-scan / eps-scan re-runs the model across k = 2…8 (elbow + silhouette) — or, for DBSCAN, plots every point's distance to its minPts-th neighbour so you can pick eps.

The profile table is where the business value lives. It shows each cluster's averages in original units, shaded by how far they sit from the overall mean. The algorithm proposes groups; you read the profile and name them.

These are starter recipes, not the only right answers. Each one has a load button that builds the pipeline for you — then change one node at a time and watch what moves. That observation is the lab.

LAB02.

Compress with PCA and LDA

Many correlated columns, a few real drivers. Build a pipeline that rewrites the data in fewer columns, then read what each new column means. Run PCA and LDA on the same labelled data — one ignores the labels, one is built on them, and the difference is the point of this lab.

📖 Lab guide — scaling for variance, PCA vs LDA, and what to combine read first ▾

PCA hunts for the directions of largest variance. Variance depends on units — so the scaling decision decides what PCA finds. Cleaning runs in order.

Standardize (z-score) Gives every column variance 1, so PCA looks at correlation structure rather than units. Without it, a ratio measured in tens (ROE %) out-votes one measured in fractions (debt/equity). Use whenever columns have different units — ratios, credit metrics, customer data.
No scaling (deliberate) When every column is in the same unit — basis-point moves on a yield curve, % returns — raw variance is meaningful: a tenor that moves more should weigh more. Defensible on the yield curve and returns data. Try both and compare the loadings.
Log-transform skew Interest cover and similar ratios have long right tails; a few extreme firms can tilt a component towards themselves. Use before scaling on skewed, positive ratios.
Drop duplicates / missing The covariance matrix needs complete rows. Duplicates double-weight a firm; nulls must be dropped or imputed. Use on the financial-ratios dataset (3 duplicates, 4 nulls).
Remove outliers (IQR) Extreme days on a return series are real market behaviour — trimming them makes risk look smaller than it is. Caution on market data. Reasonable on cross-sectional ratios if a few firms are data errors.
Normalize (min-max) Bounds each column to [0,1]; one extreme value squashes the rest, which distorts variance. Prefer z-score for PCA.
The canonical order
dedup→ drop / impute nulls→ log-transform→ scale (unless one shared unit)→ PCA / LDA

Same shape of answer — a few new axes — opposite question.

PCA · keep = number of components Eigen-decomposes the covariance matrix. PC1 is the direction of greatest spread, PC2 the greatest spread at right angles to PC1, and so on. Never looks at labels. Read the scree (how many to keep) and the loadings (what each one means).
LDA (Fisher) Uses a group label and finds the axes that push group means apart while keeping each group tight (maximises between ÷ within spread). With g groups there are at most g−1 axes. Needs a grouped dataset — Credit risk bands or Issuer ratings. It is the bridge back to supervised learning.
Reading the evaluation

Variance explained shows the scree (share of variance per component, with the cumulative line) and the projection onto the first two axes. The metric that matters is how few components keep ~80–90%.

Group separation (grouped datasets only) colours the projection by the known label and adds two numbers: the Fisher ratio (between-group ÷ within-group spread in the 2-D view) and axis-1 accuracy — how often a row sits nearest its own group's centre using the first axis alone. Run it with PCA, then LDA, on the same data.

The loadings table is where the business value lives. Every original column's weight on every new axis, shaded by sign and size, with an automatic "reads as" line: a common factor (everything moves together), a contrast (these ↑ vs those ↓), or — on the yield curve — level, slope and curvature.

Pipeline tips by method

PCA — standardize unless all columns share one unit; choose the number of components from the scree’s elbow or a 80–90% cumulative target; always name components from the loadings before using them.

LDA — needs a label; gives at most (groups − 1) axes; standardize so the weights are comparable. If you have the label and care about it, LDA beats PCA at separating it.

LAB03.

Mine association rules with Apriori

No geometry here — just co-occurrence. Build a pipeline that preps customer baskets, mines frequent itemsets, filters the rules and scores them. The trap to find: rules that are confident only because the item they predict is everywhere anyway.

📖 Lab guide — basket prep, reading a rule, and what to combine read first ▾

Basket data is categorical — each customer either holds an item or doesn't. There is no distance, so scaling does nothing (the palette includes Standardize so you can see the pipeline skip it). The preparation that matters is deciding which items and which baskets count.

Drop near-universal items (≥80%) An item almost everyone holds (a savings account, UPI, groceries) makes every rule that ends in it look highly confident — with lift ≈ 1, i.e. meaningless. Use first on all three datasets. Run once without it to see the pollution.
Drop rare items (<5%) Items too rare to reach min support only slow the search and create fragile, tiny-sample rules. Use when the catalogue is long; skip when a rare item is the product you want to sell.
Drop single-item baskets One-product customers can never form a rule, but they are part of the denominator. Dropping them raises every support and confidence figure. Caution — the rules then describe multi-product customers only. Say so when you quote the numbers.
Filtering rules afterwards
Keep lift ≥ 1.2Removes coincidences — rules no stronger than the consequent's baseline popularity.Almost always worth adding.
Single-item consequentKeeps "if X, pitch one product" rules — the form an offer engine can use.Use for next-best-offer.
Drop redundant rulesRemoves a longer rule when a shorter one with the same consequent is at least as confident.Use when the rule list is long.
drop universal items→ drop rare items→ (optional) drop single baskets→ Apriori→ filter by lift

Apriori · sup≥ · conf≥. First find every itemset held by at least min support of baskets — using the Apriori shortcut: if an item is rare, any set containing it is at least as rare, so it is never counted. Then split each frequent set into "if A → then B" rules and keep those with at least min confidence.

Support = P(A and B)How often the combination appears. Low support = too few customers to build a campaign around.Lower min support to find rarer, more specific pairings.
Confidence = P(B | A)Of the customers with A, the share who also hold B. Can lie: if B is held by 90% of everyone, 90% confidence is no information at all.Always compare against the "baseline of →" column.
Lift = confidence ÷ P(B)How many times more likely B is when A is present. Lift 1 = coincidence; above 1 = a genuine pull; below 1 = the items repel.The honest metric — rank by it.

Rule metrics reports the rules found, the top lift, and a count of coincidence rules (lift < 1.1). The charts show the top rules by lift and every rule's support vs confidence, coloured by lift — the confident-but-flat rules sit high on the chart in grey. The item-frequency chips under the table show which items are near-universal.

Cross-sell baskets — the savings trapcounter-example Product baskets → Apriori (support≥15%, confidence≥50%) → rule metrics  ·  no prep
Why: every customer holds a savings account, so it joins every frequent itemset. The run returns 25 rules — 13 of them coincidences with lift ≈ 1 ("credit card → savings", 100% confident, zero information) — and the true pairings appear three times over, with and without savings.
Cross-sell baskets — Apriori done properlyassociation Product baskets → drop near-universal items → Apriori (support≥15%, confidence≥50%) → keep lift ≥ 1.2 → rule metrics
Why: drop savings and the signal appears: demat → mutual fund (100% confidence, lift ≈ 3.0), mutual fund → demat, and credit card ⇄ insurance (lift ≈ 1.7) — four clean rules instead of 25. The 71 savings-only customers stay in the denominator: they are still customers, just not cross-sold ones. Lower min support to 5% to find the rarer three-product combinations (lift up to ≈ 5.4).
