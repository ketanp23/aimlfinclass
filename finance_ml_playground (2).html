Working notes on the seven algorithms in this playground — what each one does, the boundary it draws, the knob that controls it, where it earns its keep in finance, and how to read its evaluation. Every algorithm here is supervised: it learns from labelled examples (borrowers who did or didn't default, transactions that were or weren't fraud) to score new cases.

The shared workflow

Whatever the model, the pipeline is the same: frame the problem (is the answer a category or a number?), gather and clean point-in-time-correct data, split into train and test, fit on the training fold, evaluate on data the model has never seen, and monitor for drift once live. This playground fixes the first four steps and lets you play with the last two — every metric you see is computed on a held-out 30% test split, never the training data.

Linear SVM

A straight decision boundary with the widest possible margin.

A Support Vector Machine looks for the boundary that separates the two classes with the largest margin — the widest empty corridor between them. In its linear form that boundary is a straight line (a hyperplane in higher dimensions). Only the points nearest the boundary, the support vectors, actually determine where it sits; the rest could move freely without changing the model.

Boundarya single straight line / hyperplaneKey knobC — tolerance for margin violations (low = wide & forgiving, high = fits every point)Strengthsfast, stable, and each feature weight is a readable risk coefficientWeaknesscannot represent a curved or corner boundary — underfits non-linear problems
In finance: credit scorecards and any decision a regulator must be able to read as "so much risk per unit of each feature." The first model to reach for, and the benchmark every fancier model must beat.
Reading its evaluation

Watch it collapse on the Fraud ring and AML scenarios — a straight line can't enclose a ring, so recall falls off a cliff. When it does fit (credit, downgrade, M&A) its AUC is competitive and its weights are the bonus.

SVM with an RBF kernel

The same margin idea, bent into a curve by the kernel trick.

The kernel trick projects the data into a higher-dimensional space where a straight boundary becomes possible, then maps it back — the visible effect is a smooth, curved boundary. The Radial Basis Function (Gaussian) kernel measures similarity by distance, so the model can wrap closed curves around clusters that no line could separate.

Boundarysmooth curves, closed loops around clustersKey knobγ — how local the influence of each point is (high = tight, wiggly islands; low = broad, smooth)Strengthscaptures curved and ring-shaped patterns a line cannotWeaknessno simple per-feature explanation; high γ overfits
In finance: fraud rings and AML scatter, curved approval frontiers, compound-risk corners — anywhere the true boundary bends. The price is interpretability.
Reading its evaluation

The star on Fraud, AML and Loan approval, where its AUC jumps well above the linear model. If γ is too high, training accuracy soars while test accuracy sags — the classic overfitting signature.

Decision Tree

A flowchart of yes/no splits — every prediction is a readable rule.

A tree repeatedly splits the data on one feature at a time, each split chosen to make the resulting groups as pure (single-class) as possible — measured by Gini impurity or entropy. Following a path from root to leaf reads as a plain rule: "if match-variance > t and price-variance > s, route to review." The boundary it draws is always a set of axis-aligned rectangles.

Boundaryaxis-aligned rectangles (a staircase)Key knobmax depth — how many stacked questions (deeper = finer but overfits)Strengthsthe most interpretable model here; handles hinges and corners exactlyWeaknessa single tree is unstable — small data changes redraw it; deep trees overfit
In finance: the natural fit for policy-style rules — P2P invoice routing, GST audit hinges, transparent risk segmentation — where the decision must be documented and defended verbatim.
Reading its evaluation

Best where the true boundary is axis-aligned (P2P, GST). Push max depth up and watch the boundary turn jagged and test metrics wobble — the definition of overfitting.

Random Forest

Hundreds of decorrelated trees vote — accurate and stable.

A forest trains many trees, each on a random bootstrap sample of the rows and a random subset of features at each split, then averages their votes. Individual trees overfit in different directions, so averaging cancels their errors and produces a smooth, robust boundary — usually the strongest off-the-shelf model for tabular finance data.

Boundarya smoothed average of many staircasesKey knobnumber of trees (more = steadier) and per-tree depthStrengthshigh accuracy, resistant to overfitting, handles rare classes wellWeaknessno single readable rule — you recover drivers via feature-importance
In finance: the default workhorse for credit scoring, fraud, and churn — reached for whenever raw predictive accuracy on tabular data matters more than a one-line explanation.
Reading its evaluation

Consistently strong AUC across almost every scenario. On imbalanced problems (downgrade, AML) it ranks well but still needs a lowered threshold — accuracy alone will still mislead.

K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN)

Classify a new case by the majority vote of its closest past cases.

KNN does no training at all — the data is the model. To score a new point it finds the K most similar past cases by distance and takes their majority label (or average probability). Because "similar" means distance, features must be on comparable scales or the largest-magnitude one dominates.

Boundarylocal and naturally curved, follows the dataKey knobK — neighbours polled (small = jumpy/local, large = smooth/toward the base rate)Strengthssimple, captures curves and corners, shows the actual comparable casesWeaknessslow to predict at scale; struggles on rare classes and needs scaling
In finance: peer-comparable valuation ("find the five most similar deals; their average multiple is the fair value") and distance-based novelty flags in anomaly detection.
Reading its evaluation

Good on curved and scattered problems (fraud, AML) because neighbours capture local shape; weaker on heavily imbalanced data, where a new case's neighbours are usually the majority class.

Naïve Bayes

Probabilistic scoring that assumes features are independent.

Gaussian Naïve Bayes fits a simple bell curve to each feature within each class, then multiplies those per-feature probabilities together (with the class base rate) to get a score. The "naïve" assumption — that features are independent given the class — is rarely exactly true, yet the model is fast, needs little data, and is a surprisingly strong baseline.

Boundarysmooth, essentially elliptical / quadraticKey knobnone — it fits directly from the dataStrengthsextremely fast, works with little data, a solid first baselineWeaknessthe independence assumption breaks on correlated features and interactions
In finance: first-pass fraud triage, document and email classification — anywhere a cheap, instant probability is more valuable than a perfectly calibrated one.
Reading its evaluation

Strong where classes are roughly bell-shaped (credit, downgrade, AML's tight normal cloud); structurally weak on interactions (policy lapse) and "OR" rules (loan approval), where independence fails.

One-Class SVM

Learns the shape of "normal" from normal data alone; flags the rest.

Unlike the others, this is trained on a single class — normal behaviour only — and draws a boundary around it. Anything falling outside is flagged as an anomaly, no anomaly labels required. That is exactly what you want when the "bad" cases are rare, diverse, and unlabelled, and when tomorrow's anomaly may look nothing like yesterday's.

Boundarya closed region around normal dataKey knobγ (boundary tightness) and ν — the fraction of normal data you accept flagging (your alert budget)Strengthsneeds no anomaly labels; generalises to unseen anomaly typesWeaknessonly as good as the definition of "normal"; ν must be tuned to review capacity
In finance: AML transaction monitoring and novelty / first-party-fraud detection — the natural tool whenever you can describe normal but not enumerate every abnormal.
Reading its evaluation

Judge it on recall against alert volume, not accuracy. ν is the direct lever: raise it to catch more and alert more, lower it to protect analyst time.

Reading the metrics

Every classification metric is a ratio of four counts.

Once a threshold turns each probability into a yes/no call, every prediction on the test set falls into one of four boxes — the confusion matrix. True positives and true negatives are correct; false positives are false alarms; false negatives are misses. Which of the two error types hurts more is a business question, and it decides which metric you optimise.

MetricWhat it answersLean on it when
AccuracyWhat share of all calls were right?the classes are roughly balanced — misleading when they aren't
PrecisionOf the cases I flagged, how many were real?a false alarm is costly (blocking a good customer, wasting an auditor)
RecallOf the real positives, how many did I catch?a miss is costly (missed fraud, downgrade, or SAR filing)
F1The harmonic mean of precision and recall.you need one number and both errors matter
AUC-ROCHow well does the model rank positives above negatives?no threshold is fixed yet, or it varies by policy — threshold-free ranking quality
The accuracy trap

If only 5% of cases are positive, a model that predicts "negative" for everyone is 95% accurate and catches nothing. On every imbalanced scenario here (fraud, AML, downgrade, M&A breaks) accuracy flatters a useless model — which is why the panel highlights recall, F1, or AUC instead, and marks the metric to watch.

The threshold is a policy, not a model output

A classifier outputs a probability; you choose the cut-off that turns it into an action. Slide it down to catch more positives (higher recall, more false alarms); slide it up to act only on the surest cases (higher precision, more misses). The model doesn't change — the business's appetite for each error does. That is the slider you're moving in the playground.
