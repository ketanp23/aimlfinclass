PARTI.

Why exchange rates are the hardest thing to forecast

Currencies are the deep end of prediction. The reason is not that the maths is hard — it is that the target is almost pure noise, and the first models you try will lie to you about how well they are doing.

THE CORE IDEA An exchange rate behaves like a random walk: tomorrow ≈ today, plus an unpredictable shock. The shock is most of the move.

The random-walk starting point

Meese and Rogoff showed in 1983 that sophisticated economic models could not out-forecast a trivial rule — “tomorrow’s rate equals today’s rate” — at short horizons. Forty years on, that result still stands as the benchmark every FX model is measured against. The reason is the efficient-market logic: if a piece of public information reliably predicted the rate, traders would act on it now, and the move would already be in today’s price. What is left over for tomorrow is mostly noise.

So the honest framing of the problem is not “predict the exchange rate.” It is: “can I beat the random walk, even slightly, out of sample, after being honest about costs?” Usually the answer is no. Sometimes — with the right variables and modest expectations — it is a thin yes.

Level versus return — the distinction everything hinges on

You can frame the forecast two ways, and they behave completely differently:

predict the level:   ratet+1   vs.   predict the return:   rt+1 = (ratet+1 − ratet) / ratet The level is the price itself; the return is the daily change. A model of the level looks brilliant and teaches you nothing. A model of the return looks terrible and is the only honest test.

Predicting the level is deceptively easy. Because tomorrow’s rate is almost always within a whisker of today’s, any model that stays near the current price scores a huge R² — 0.95 and up. But so does the do-nothing random walk. The R² measures the size of the daily move relative to the whole five-year range; it does not measure skill.

Predicting the return strips that illusion away. Now the target is just the daily wiggle, which is mostly noise, so R² collapses toward zero — for every model, including good ones. This is uncomfortable but honest, and it is where real FX modelling lives.

The R² trap

A 0.96 R² on the level is not a good model — it is the baseline. The random walk gets it for free. Report skill against that baseline, never the raw R², or you will fool yourself and your risk committee.

PARTII.

The features — what could possibly predict a currency

If there is any signal at all, it comes from a handful of macro drivers and from the rate’s own recent behaviour. None is strong; together they may add up to a thin edge.

A NOTE Every feature is known at the close of day t and used to predict day t+1. Using anything from t+1 is look-ahead leakage — the classic way to build a model that works only in the backtest.

The drivers in this dataset

Each row is one trading day. The predictors fall into two families. Macro drivers — the interest-rate carry (India’s repo rate minus the US funds rate), the daily move in the dollar index, the move in crude oil (India imports most of its oil, so costlier crude weakens the rupee), and net foreign equity flows. Price-derived signals — yesterday’s return (momentum), the gap between the rate and its 20-day average (mean reversion), and recent realized volatility.

The economics fix the signs. A stronger dollar or costlier oil pushes ₹/$ up (rupee weakens); a wider carry and heavier inflows pull it down (rupee strengthens). A model that learns the wrong sign is overfitting to noise.

Stationarity — why we difference

The level trends and wanders (it is non-stationary), which is exactly why distance- and tree-based models fail on it: they cannot predict a value outside the range they were trained on. Returns are roughly stationary — they hover around zero with stable spread — so almost all serious FX modelling is done on returns, not levels.

PARTIII.

How you actually grade an FX forecast

Accuracy and R² are the wrong headline numbers here. Three measures tell the truth: a time-ordered test, skill against the random walk, and directional accuracy.

NEVER SHUFFLE A currency series is ordered in time. Shuffle it into a random train/test split and you train on the future to predict the past — the results look wonderful and mean nothing.

1 · Split by time, not at random

Train on the earlier part of the history, test on the later part you have never touched. This mimics reality: you only ever have the past to forecast the future. A random split leaks tomorrow into today’s training set and is the single most common way backtests lie.

2 · Skill score — always versus the random walk

skill = 1 − MSEmodel / MSErandom walk Positive means you beat “tomorrow = today.” Zero means you tied it. Negative — very common — means your clever model is worse than doing nothing. This one number cuts through the R² illusion.

Because the random walk is such a strong benchmark on levels, a model can post a 0.96 R² and a negative skill score at the same time. The skill score is what a trading desk actually cares about.

3 · Directional accuracy — did we call the move?

For many desks, getting the direction right matters more than the exact number: you hedge or you don’t. Directional accuracy asks what fraction of days the model got the sign of the move right. Fifty percent is a coin flip. Anything reliably above ~53–55% out of sample is a genuine — and rare — edge.

Putting it together

A useful FX model shows positive skill and directional accuracy above 50% on a time-ordered test set — and stays modest about all three. In the lab you will see naive baselines, honest small edges, and models that look great on R² while failing on skill. Learn to read the third number, not the first.

CASE★

The treasury desk that needed a number

Before the maths, the business. Meet the person who has to act on the forecast, the decision it drives, and why she reads the skill score and not the R².

RS
Rina Shah
Group Treasurer · Kaveri Textiles Ltd

She owns the company’s currency risk — and the P&L consequences of every hedging call.

The company. Kaveri Textiles exports home furnishings to the US and imports cotton and dyes priced in dollars. Roughly $4 million flows across its books each month. When the rupee moves a rupee against the dollar, several lakh of margin appears or evaporates — money the mill never earned or lost operationally.

The problem. Rina must decide, each week, how much of the next quarter’s dollar exposure to hedge forward. Hedge too much and she pays away forward points and locks out favourable moves; hedge too little and a sudden rupee slide eats the year’s margin. Her CFO keeps asking the impossible question: “where will USD/INR be next month?”

The temptation. A vendor pitches an ML model that “predicts USD/INR with 96% accuracy.” It sounds like the answer to her problem. It is not — and understanding why is the whole point of this case. That 96% is an R² on the level, and the do-nothing random walk scores the same. The model has no skill; it has a statistic that flatters it.

What actually helps her. Not a false promise of the exact rate, but an honest, small edge on direction: a model that, using carry, the dollar index and oil, calls next week’s move correctly a bit more than half the time, with a positive skill score on a time-ordered test. That is enough to tilt her hedge ratio — hedge a little more when the model leans toward a weaker rupee — while sizing the bet to the model’s modest confidence.

The decision the model feeds

Rina never trades on a point forecast of the level. She reads the direction and the skill score, adjusts the quarter’s hedge ratio by a few percentage points, and re-checks weekly whether the edge is still there. A model whose skill has decayed to zero goes back in the drawer — and the random-walk hedge (hedge a steady fraction) takes over again.

Predict returns, not the level

The level forecast flatters every model. Rina’s analysts model the daily return, where the honest difficulty shows.

Beat the random walk or go home

The benchmark is “tomorrow = today.” If the model can’t beat it out of sample, it adds nothing to the hedge decision.

Direction > precision

She hedges or she doesn’t. Calling the sign right 55% of the time is worth more than a precise-looking level that’s really just today’s price.
